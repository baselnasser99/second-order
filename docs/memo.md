# Memo: Why Olist customers don't come back

**To:** Olist leadership
**From:** Basel Nasser
**Date:** October 2026
**Based on:** Olist orders from 2016 to 2018, the Second Order dashboard and `docs/metrics.md`

## Bottom line

Olist's biggest loss isn't bad orders. It's that almost nobody orders a second time. Only about 3 in every 100 people (3.12%, or 2,997 of 96,096) ever placed a second order. The data shows no clear difference in return rates between people whose first order arrived late and people whose order was on time. Even if late delivery does make people less likely to return, only about 6 in 100 first orders were late, too few to move the overall rate much. So faster delivery alone is unlikely to bring many people back.

## What we found

**1. Customers rarely return, and the data shows no clear difference between late and on-time first orders.** Of the 56,007 people we could follow for a full 180 days after their first order, 1,116 came back within that time (1.99%). People whose first order arrived late came back slightly less often than people whose order was on time (1.69% of 3,317 vs 2.02% of 50,681), but a gap that small could be chance: its 95% confidence interval runs from −0.12 to +0.79 percentage points, which includes zero. Late first orders are too few (6.14%) to matter much: if every one had returned at the on-time rate, the overall rate would rise by only about 0.05 percentage points, even at the top of that range. Return rates by first-order review score, from 1 to 5, were all between 1.5% and 2.2%. Even happy first-time customers rarely return.

**2. The most late orders come from large sellers, not the top of the ranking.** 6.77% of delivered orders arrived late. The sellers with the highest late rates are small: the top one has 14 late orders. One large seller with a 9.71% late rate has 172 late orders, more than the 10 highest-rate sellers combined (140).

**3. Customers paid 269,735.11 BRL on 1,234 orders that were canceled or unavailable.** The data doesn't show refunds, so this is money at risk, not a confirmed loss. Of the 609 unavailable orders, 603 have no items recorded, so the data can't say what the customer was buying.

## What to fix first

**1. Test ways to win the second order.** For example, offer a follow-up deal to half of new customers and compare their 180-day return rate with the other half. Today's rate, 1.99%, is the baseline. Faster delivery alone is unlikely to move it much (finding 1). It is also worth asking whether customers know they bought from Olist at all: Olist sells through other marketplaces, so buyers may remember the marketplace instead. This data can't answer that question, but a short customer survey could.

**2. Start delivery work with the sellers that have the most late orders.** The top one alone has more than the ten highest-rate sellers combined, so fixing its delivery times would remove more late orders than fixing those ten together. Judge progress by the number of late orders as well as the rate.

**3. Find out what happened to the 269,735.11 BRL.** Check refund records for the 1,234 canceled and unavailable orders, starting with the 603 unavailable orders that have no items.

## Reading the money numbers

Customers paid 16,008,872.12 BRL in total, more than the 13,591,643.70 BRL order value (item prices only). Most of the gap is freight (2,251,909.54 BRL), which order value leaves out. Another 162,591.95 BRL was paid on 775 orders with no items. The last 2,726.93 BRL is small differences such as installment interest.

## Limits

- The data covers 2016 to 2018-08-21. It shows patterns, not causes: it can show whether late orders and returns go together, not whether one causes the other.
- The data ends in 2018, so the recommendations are what Olist could have tested then, not current advice.
- 180 days may be short for products people rarely rebuy, such as furniture.
- It has no refunds, so payments on canceled orders can't be called losses.
- It has no seller names, only seller ID codes.
